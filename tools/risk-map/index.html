    Which national park has the most missing hikers?

    In the compiled public-record dataset behind this map, Yosemite National Park has by far the most documented missing-person cases (39), followed by Great Smoky Mountains (9) and Grand Canyon (8). Death Valley, Olympic and Yellowstone each have 5.

    These are compiled public reports for safety awareness, not official or live National Park Service totals. Busy parks see more cases partly because they get millions of visitors, and the terrain that makes them spectacular (granite, canyons, glaciers, dense forest) is the same terrain that hides people and blocks phone signal.

    Why do experienced hikers go missing near trailheads?

    Most people who get lost are not far from where they started. Experienced hikers take shortcuts or side trails, follow game trails or drainages that look like the route, push past a planned turnaround time, or get caught by fast-changing weather or darkness.

    • Ridge dead zones: cell signal often disappears within minutes of the trailhead behind ridges and in valleys.
    • Sudden weather: afternoon storms, fog and early dark turn an easy return into a slow, cold one.
    • Boulder and timber terrain: talus, blowdown and dense forest look the same in every direction.
    • Late turnarounds: "just a bit further" leaves too little daylight for the trip back.

    If nobody knows the route and the return time, the search starts late and in the wrong place. That is the problem a written trip plan solves.

    Does phone GPS work without cell service in national parks?

    Yes. Your phone's GPS receiver listens to satellites, not cell towers, so it can show your position on maps you downloaded in advance and record a GPS trail with no signal. Calls, texts, live location sharing and new map tiles all need a network. iPhone 14 and later can reach emergency services by satellite in supported countries, and a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon can call for help from anywhere. Read more in solo hiking safety without cell service.

    Are these official National Park Service numbers?

    No. The counts reflect compiled public-record reports of people who went missing in or near National Parks, National Forests and other wilderness areas, gathered for safety awareness. They are not official, complete or live National Park Service, Forest Service or search-and-rescue dispatch totals, and a higher count does not mean a place is unsafe to visit. Coordinates are approximate area centres, not last-seen points.

    How do I stay safe hiking alone in a high-cluster park?

    Leave a written trip plan with someone you trust that includes your route, your return time and a deadline for calling for help. Set a turnaround time with the hiking time calculator and keep it, download offline maps, carry a headlamp and a layer for an unplanned night, and in remote areas carry a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon. Looking for whether a Missing 411 mobile app or official map exists? Read our Missing 411 app and interactive map guide.

    About the data. This map uses an open, public-record compilation of missing-person cases in and near U.S. national parks and wilderness areas (published on Kaggle as "Open Missing People Cases Inside National Parks"), cleaned by Don't Go Missing: names standardised, records for the same area merged, and a handful of clearly mis-located records corrected or removed. Classifications are based only on the case count: High-Cluster Zone (5 or more), Documented SAR Zone (2 to 4) and Recorded Case Zone (1). The data is also available as JSON. Spotted an error?
