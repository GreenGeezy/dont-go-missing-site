How to Leave a Hiking Trip Plan (With a Free Template)

By the Don't Go Missing team · Updated November 16, 2026 · 6 minute read

The short answer: a hiking trip plan is a written note you give to someone you trust before you leave. It says where you're going, when you'll be back, and the exact time they should call for help if they haven't heard from you. It takes five minutes, and it's what lets a search start quickly and in the right place.

Most hikers already do a version of this. They tell a partner "I'm doing the lake trail, back for dinner." The problem is everything that sentence leaves out. Which lake trail? Which trailhead? What time is dinner, and at what point should anyone actually pick up the phone?

A trip plan closes those gaps. This guide covers what goes in one, who should get it, and how to make sure it works on the day you need it.

What is a hiking trip plan?

A hiking trip plan is a short document, often just a text message, that records your destination, route, timing and a deadline for raising the alarm. You leave it with someone who isn't coming along. Boaters call the same thing a float plan, and pilots file a flight plan for the same reason: if you don't arrive, someone knows where to start looking.

What should a hiking trip plan include?

Include these eight things. The first four are essential; the rest make a search faster.

  1. Who is going. Your name and phone number, and the name of everyone in your group.
  2. Where you're going. The destination, the trailhead you're starting from, and the route you plan to take. Name the trail, not just the area.
  3. When you'll be back. A clock time, not "this evening."
  4. The deadline. The exact time your contact should call for help. This is the most important line in the plan and the one most people leave out.
  5. Your backup plan. What you'll do if the weather turns or the route is closed, so nobody searches the wrong valley.
  6. Your vehicle. Make, colour and plate, and where it's parked. A car still sitting at a trailhead confirms you haven't come out.
  7. What you look like and carry. Jacket and pack colours, and whether you have a shelter, headlamp, extra food and water.
  8. Medical information. Conditions, medications or allergies a responder should know about.

A trip plan template you can copy

Paste this into a text or email and fill in the brackets. Or use the free trip plan generator, which builds it for you and lets you text, email or print it.

TRIP PLAN: you are my safety contact for this trip. Who: [your name, phone number] With: [solo / names] Destination: [trail and area] Starting from: [trailhead or coordinates] Route: [planned route, and backup if conditions change] Leaving: [day, time] Back by: [day, time] Vehicle: [make, colour, plate, where parked] Wearing / carrying: [jacket and pack colours, key gear] Notes: [medical or anything else] If you have not heard from me by [day, time], call or text me first. If you cannot reach me, call the local emergency number and read them this plan. I'll message you as soon as I'm out.

Who should you give your trip plan to?

Give it to one or two people who meet three tests:

  • They're not on the trip. If they're with you, they're in the same situation you are.
  • They'll notice. Someone who will actually look at the clock that evening.
  • They'll act. Someone willing to make an uncomfortable phone call rather than wait until morning.

Send it in writing and get a reply. A plan your contact hasn't read is the same as no plan.

When should your contact call for help?

Set a specific time before you leave, and put it in the plan. For a day hike, one to two hours after your planned return is a common choice. For a multi-day trip, people often allow longer, sometimes until the following morning. What matters is that it's a clock time you both agreed on, so your contact isn't left wondering whether it's too early to worry.

To choose a realistic return time in the first place, estimate the hike with the hiking time and turnaround calculator, then add a margin.

If someone is overdue: in the United States, call 911 and say you are reporting an overdue hiker. In most areas the county sheriff runs search and rescue; in national parks it's park dispatch. Give them the plan. You don't need to wait 24 hours.

How to leave a trip plan, step by step

  1. Pick your contact and ask them, so they know they have the job.
  2. Write down where and when: destination, trailhead, route, group, leaving time, return time.
  3. Set the deadline for calling for help.
  4. Add the details for searchers: vehicle, clothing, gear, medical notes.
  5. Send it and confirm they've seen it before you lose signal.
  6. Check out when you're back. Message them the moment you're out.

That last step is what keeps the system honest. If you sometimes forget to check out, your contact learns that silence is normal, and the deadline stops meaning anything.

Common mistakes

  • Changing the plan without telling anyone. If you switch trails at the trailhead, send an update before you start walking.
  • No deadline. "Back around six" gives your contact nothing to act on at eight.
  • Relying on live location. It freezes when you lose signal and can't say that you're late.
  • Cutting the return time too fine. Leave room for a slow descent, a long lunch or a wrong turn. An early return is fine; a false alarm is not.
  • Skipping it for "easy" hikes. Familiar trails close to home are exactly where people stop bothering.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a trip plan for a short day hike?

Yes. Short, familiar hikes are where people most often skip a plan, and a rolled ankle a mile from the car is still a problem if nobody knows you're out. For a short hike the plan can be one text message with the trail, your return time and a deadline.

Is leaving a note in my car enough?

No. A note on the dashboard helps searchers once a search has started, but it doesn't start one. Someone has to notice you're missing, which means a person needs the plan and the deadline.

Who do I call if someone is overdue from a hike?

In the United States, call 911 and say you are reporting an overdue hiker. In most areas the county sheriff runs search and rescue; in national parks it is park dispatch. Give them the trip plan: where the person was going, when they were due back, their vehicle and what they were wearing.

Should I share my live location instead of a trip plan?

Use it as well, not instead. Live location stops updating when you lose signal and it doesn't tell anyone when you were due back. A trip plan works with no signal because your contact already has the information.
